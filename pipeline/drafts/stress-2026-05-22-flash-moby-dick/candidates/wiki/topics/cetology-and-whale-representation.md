# Cetology And Whale Representation

The novel treats cetology as a field built on broken fragments and stubborn contradictions. Ishmael’s own attempt to sort whales into a bibliographic system is offered openly as a “draught of a draught,” a sketch that can never close the gap between living creatures and human classes. In the chapter “Cetology,” he announces the project as “the classification of the constituents of a chaos,” setting an intentionally hopeless task.

That condition is prefigured by the “Extracts” opening the narrative. A self-deprecating “Sub-Sub-Librarian” compiles a “higgledy-piggledy” collection of whale references from sacred and profane sources, and Ishmael warns that these are not “veritable gospel cetology.” The compilation shows that generations of authorities have only produced “utter confusion.” Cuvier, John Hunter, and Lesson lament fields “strewn with thorns” and “incomplete indications” that “torture us naturalists,” positioning cetology as a discipline whose primary object withdraws from systematic capture.

The whale’s body itself resists stable representation. Its spout remains a scientific riddle, its brain is a tiny kernel inside a bone‑battering mass, and its precious products—fragrant ambergris, pure spermaceti—emerge from foul decay. The quality of whiteness intensifies this elusiveness, divorcing the animal from benign definition and turning it toward a void explored in [[whiteness-and-terror-in-moby-dick]]. Ahab, meanwhile, treats the White Whale not as a mere animal but as the “pasteboard mask” concealing cosmic malevolence, making the whale a kind of representation that drives his monomaniacal chase (see [[ahab-and-the-white-whale]]). Every attempt to know or picture the whale ultimately restates the distance between human categories and the “unshored, harbourless immensities” of the deep.
