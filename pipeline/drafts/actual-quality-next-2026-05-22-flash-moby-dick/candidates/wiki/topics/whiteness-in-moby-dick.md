# Whiteness In Moby Dick

In "The Whiteness of the Whale," Ishmael traces the peculiar dread that the white whale stirs in him—a terror distinct from the creature's size or ferocity. He surveys whiteness across cultures and finds it universally honored: white elephants confer royal status, white robes dignify the redeemed in Revelation, and white ermine symbolizes judicial majesty. Yet for all its associations with purity, gladness, and authority, whiteness harbors an undercurrent that unsettles more deeply than the redness of blood. When separated from benevolent meanings and joined to something already fearsome, white intensifies horror to its limit; the polar bear and the great white shark owe their shuddering effect not to fang or claw but to a blanched, spectral surface that repels through its very mildness. Ishmael's meditation ultimately confronts whiteness as an absence of colour—a blankness that suggests the dissolution of all distinctions, an intimation of the void. What Moby Dick represents to [[ahab-vengeance-and-the-white-whale|Ahab]] as a personal enemy becomes, for Ishmael, a glimpse into something more annihilating: the suspicion that behind the visible world lies not malevolence but vacancy.
