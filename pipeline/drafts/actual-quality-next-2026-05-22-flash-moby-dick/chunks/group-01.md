Narratively, Ishmael introduces himself as a man who goes to sea to escape melancholy and chooses a whaling voyage out of a fate‑driven fascination with the great whale. He arrives in New Bedford, lodges at the Spouter‑Inn, and, after initial terror, befriends the tattooed cannibal harpooneer Queequeg. The two travel to Nantucket, sign aboard the weathered *Pequod* under the joint ownership of the Quaker captains Peleg and Bildad, and meet the ship’s mates (Starbuck, Stubb, Flask) and harpooneers (Queequeg, Tashtego, Daggoo). Ahab, the absent captain, is first described as a grand, ungodly, god‑like figure with a missing leg, a livid scar, and a “crucifixion in his face.” Once at sea, Ahab appears on deck, rebukes Stubb, discards his pipe, and shouts the first order about a white whale. The narrative breaks into a defence of whaling (“The Advocate”), a meditation on the soul and the sea (“The Lee Shore”), and the beginning of a systematic (but chaotic) classification of whales.

## Repeated Or Central Claims
- **Ishmael’s existential reasoning**: Going to sea is an alternative to suicide or violence; whaling is chosen not for commerce but for the overwhelming idea of the whale and as a decree of the Fates.
- **Dignity and historical importance of whaling**: Whalemen are “fighting Quakers” and pioneers; whale‑ships opened the Pacific, discovered Australia, fed early colonists, and will open Japan. Whale products illuminate the world, and sperm oil may anoint monarchs.
- **Ahab’s hidden wound and obsession**: Though physically absent for several chapters, Ahab is constructed as a suffering, moody, and pre‑ordained figure. His lost leg, scar, insomnia, and symbolic acts (discarding the pipe) foreshadow a “bloody” quest tied to a monstrous white whale.
- **Queequeg and the blurring of civilised and savage**: Queequeg’s polite cannibalism, idolatry, and courage repeatedly challenge Ishmael’s (and the reader’s) assumptions. Ishmael’s moral calculus—“Better sleep with a sober cannibal than a drunken Christian”—and his Golden‑Rule justification for joining Queequeg’s worship are emblematic of the book’s relativism.
- **Fatalism and the unknowable**: From Elijah’s cryptic warnings to Bulkington’s “lee shore” and the “Isolatoes” crew, the text frames the voyage as a confrontation with forces beyond human control or full comprehension. The Cetology digression introduces systematic knowledge only to underscore its failure.
- **Economic and contractual detail**: The lay system (Ishmael’s 300th, Queequeg’s 90th) and the ship’s material preparation (spare boats, harpoons, stores) root the high symbolism in the practical business of 19th‑century whaling.
