# Cetology And Whale Lore In Moby Dick

The cetological project announced in Chapter 32 does not begin with firm definitions but with a candid confession of disorder. Ishmael admits that what he attempts is “some systematized exhibition of the whale in his broad genera,” yet the raw material resists arrangement—it is the classification of chaos. He gathers the fragmentary observations of ancient authors, natural philosophers, and whaling surgeons, only to find them strewn with thorns and wrapped in an “impenetrable veil.” The Extracts supplied by the Sub‑Sub‑Librarian reinforce this predicament: they are a promiscuous, higgledy‑piggledy anthology of lore, not gospel cetology, demonstrating that every culture has spoken of the whale but none has grasped it whole.

Faced with the impossibility of a true inner knowledge, Ishmael builds a deliberately external system. He divides whales by their visible bulk, treating them as books in folio, octavo, and duodecimo, a parody of erudition that simultaneously mocks and honours the labour of classification. The sperm whale, the “Right Whale,” and the fin‑back are graded by outward form while their inner nature remains unfathomed. This taxonomic scaffolding never closes; the living sperm whale can never be inspected entire out of water, and its spout, its skin, and its colossal, featureless face remain signs of an inaccessible depth. Even the celebrated monograph on the whale’s skeleton is left unfinished, a further embodiment of the claim that real knowledge of cetaceans is forever fugitive.

The lore that accumulates around the whale is as elusive as the science. From the plaintive Sub‑Sub’s global harvesting of sacred and profane texts, a patchwork emerges in which Leviathan is at once angel, monster, commodity, and divine riddle. The white whale in particular accrues a legendary dread, its very hue becoming the centre of a separate meditation that links whiteness to terror, absence, and the void. By gathering omens, old‑sailor tales, and scriptural echoes, the narrative layers a folk cartography over the blank places of the ocean charts, so that the whale swims through a double element: the physical sea and a sea of inherited, unverifiable story.

This resistance to representation is not an accident of incomplete data but a structural feature of the whale as Ishmael imagines it. The creature’s inscrutability turns it into a kind of dark mirror for the Pequod’s own quest. Ahab sees all visible things as pasteboard masks hiding a malevolent power, and the white whale becomes the ultimate face behind the mask. Yet even as cetology and lore promise ever‑narrower approach, the novel insists that what is glimpsed from whaleboats—a lifted fluke, a fountain of vapor, a broad back sheathed in glistening blubber—is always only a broken trace of something that permanently eludes capture, whether by harpoon or by pen.
