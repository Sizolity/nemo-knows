## Repeated Or Central Claims

- The whale is a creature of immense cultural, historical, and symbolic weight, attested by sources from Genesis to Cuvier, and its pursuit is a dignified, world‑shaping enterprise (the prefatory extracts, the Advocate chapter, the cetology introduction).
- Ishmael frames his whaling journey as a philosophical alternative to despair: he goes to sea “whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul” and regards the water‑gazing instinct as a universal human trait.
- Whaling is defended as a pioneer of exploration, commerce, and empire (it opened Pacific colonies, contributed to South American liberation, and settled Australia), and its perils are no more degrading than those of war.
- Queequeg embodies the paradox of the “noble savage”: he is a cannibal, idolater, and head‑peddler yet possesses innate politeness, “Socratic wisdom,” and a capacity for deep friendship, forcing Ishmael to re‑evaluate religious and cultural prejudice.
- Ishmael’s pragmatic tolerance is articulated in his decision to sleep with a “sober cannibal” rather than a drunken Christian, and later in his redefinition of worship as doing God’s will through love of neighbor, allowing him to join Queequeg’s idol‑worship.
- The Quaker whalemen of Nantucket (Peleg and Bildad) embody a cultural contradiction: pious non‑violence toward humans coexists with brutal violence toward whales and rigid task‑mastery; Ishmael suggests religion and business are simply compartmentalised.
- Ahab is introduced as a figure of ominous, almost tragic grandeur before he is even seen. He is a “grand, ungodly, god‑like man,” moody and withered by his maiming, with a name Peleg denies carries prophetic force even as descriptions hint otherwise; the moment he emerges he is already obsessively searching for a white whale.
- The “lee shore” emerges as a controlling metaphor for the soul’s perilous temptation to retreat to the safety of land; true independence and integrity reside in the “landlessness” of the open sea.
- Whalemen are described as “Isolatoes”—each mentally residing on a continent of his own, bound together only by the shared voyage—a portrait reinforced by the harpooneers’ diverse origins (South Seas, Gay‑Head, Africa) and the officers’ distinct personalities.
- The field of cetology is presented as chaotic and unsettled, setting up the narrator’s own ambitious (if idiosyncratic) attempt at systematic classification.
