## Repeated Or Central Claims
- **Ishmael’s motivation**: He goes to sea as a simple sailor to escape depression and suicidal ideation; the sea exerts a universal pull, figured through the myth of Narcissus.
- **Whaling as grand, imperial, and civilising**: Ishmael repeatedly ties whaling to global exploration, economic power, and royal/scriptural dignity; Nantucketers are sovereigns of the sea.
- **Pious exterior vs. violent practice**: The “fighting Quakers” Peleg and Bildad are devout in speech yet ruthless in whaling; Bildad quotes Scripture while being miserly and blood‑spattered. Father Mapple’s sermon blends social critique with theology.
- **The *Pequod* as a melancholy, ornamented vessel**: Described as a “cannibal of a craft,” old, decorated with whale bone and teeth, a noble yet gloomy ship.
- **Ahab’s hidden, maimed authority**: Ahab is repeatedly described before he appears: his leg devoured by a whale, his name prophetic, a “grand, ungodly, god‑like man.” His first appearance is silent, scarred, crucifixion‑like, and he is already called “Old Thunder.” His psychological hold over the crew appears immediately—Stubb’s dream turns a kick into an honor.
- **Universal fellowship and religious tolerance**: Ishmael works out a relativistic acceptance of Queequeg’s idol worship by redefining worship as the Golden Rule. He later persuades the Quaker owners to ship a “cannibal” by invoking a universal “First Congregational Church.”
- **Isolation and federation**: The crew are “Isolatoes”—federated outcasts—yet a democratic dignity radiates from God through all men. This paradox sits alongside Ahab’s looming tyranny.
- **Cetology as an ordered system born from chaos**: Ishmael notes the confusion of whale taxonomy and announces his own system, foreshadowing the novel’s encyclopedic drive.
