## Repeated Or Central Claims

- **Whaling as a noble and world‑shaping enterprise**: Ishmael advocates fiercely for the dignity of whaling, linking it to exploration, politics, and even royal coronation oil. Whalemen opened the Pacific, helped liberate South America, and sustained Australia.
- **Ishmael’s universalism**: He argues that all human beings belong to the “First Congregational Church” and values love of one’s neighbour over doctrinal form, enabling him to join Queequeg’s idol‑worship without guilt. At the same time, he dismisses extreme religious penances as unhealthy, blaming indigestion for hell‑thoughts.
- **The “fighting Quaker” paradox**: Peleg and Bildad embody Nantucket’s blend of devout Quaker speech and bloody whaling; Bildad quotes Scripture to justify a miserly lay while having shed “tuns of leviathan gore.”
- **Ahab as a towering, tormented figure**: Descriptions from Peleg and from his first appearance converge on a “grand, ungodly, god‑like man,” branded by suffering, with a cruciform scar and an ivory leg; his hidden agony and refusal to find solace in his pipe mark him as an anti‑hero whose will is “determinate, unsurrenderable.”
- **The sea as a realm of truth and independence**: Bulkington’s lee‑shore metaphor presents the land as the ship’s greatest danger, while the open sea is the soul’s true, perilous home. Deep thinking is in “drag” toward that open independence.
- **Queequeg’s dignity beneath a “savage” exterior**: He is repeatedly shown to possess innate civility, honesty, and a Socratic wisdom that surpasses Christian hypocrisy. Despite being a cannibal and idol‑worshipper, he earns a high lay and becomes Ishmael’s intimate friend.
- **Portents of disaster**: Elijah’s cryptic warnings, the memorial tablets in the Whaleman’s Chapel, and Ahab’s hidden torment all converge to foretell a fateful voyage.
