## What It Is
A plain-text retrieval of Herman Melville’s novel *Moby-Dick*, captured from Project Gutenberg as a corpus item. The source begins with YAML administrative metadata and then presents the entire novel, from the front matter (title page, “Etymology,” “Extracts”) through the final chapters.

## Summary
Ishmael, a reflective sailor, recounts his decision to go whaling, his friendship with the noble harpooneer Queequeg, and his voyage on the whaleship *Pequod* under the monomaniac Captain Ahab. Ahab reveals that the ship’s true quarry is the white whale Moby Dick, who took his leg, and binds the crew to his vengeance through a ritual oath. The narrative weaves together cetological digressions, the ambivalent symbol of whiteness, shark‑torn carcasses, a Grand Armada of sperm whales, the monkey‑rope of human interdependence, the castaway Pip, and encounters with ships like the *Jeroboam*, *Jungfrau*, and *Bouton‑de‑Rose*. Philosophical meditations on the sea, fate, knowledge, and the limits of classification run through the action, blending tragedy with coarse humour, detailed whaling procedure, and a constant awareness of the whale’s unreadable mystery. The source contains the complete text as retrieved, without abridgment.

## Key Claims
- The sperm whale is the true monarch of the seas, yet its full nature and intelligence cannot be captured by science, art, or language; all cetological systems are provisional and incomplete.
- Ahab’s pursuit of Moby Dick is a metaphysical rebellion against a “pasteboard mask” of visible reality; the white whale embodies either an inscrutable malice or a blank wall, and Ahab’s monomania fuses his soul with cunning vengeance.
- Whiteness functions as an ambivalent force—sacred, royal, and beautiful, yet also a “nameless horror” suggestive of absence, annihilation, and heartless voids, which intensifies the whale’s terror beyond physical danger.
- The crew is bound by ritual and a “sympathetical feeling” to share Ahab’s quest despite private misgivings (Starbuck’s moral objection, Stubb’s fatalistic laughter, Pip’s fear).
- Whaling is portrayed as a historically vital and dignified enterprise (exploration, economics, coronation oil) but also as a brutal calculus that reduces majestic creatures to product and can measure a man’s life against the price of whale oil.
- Human interdependence is a universal law, exemplified by the monkey‑rope that physically ties men together during flensing, and by the “Fast‑Fish and Loose‑Fish” principle extended satirically to property, empire, and marriage.
- The whale’s body is an unreadable text—its brow a Sphynx, its true brain minuscule and hidden, its spout a hypothesis, and its blubber carrying indecipherable marks—reinforcing the limits of human knowledge.
- Superstition, fanaticism, and false prophecy (Gabriel’s warnings, Stubb’s suspicion of Fedallah as devil) coexist with Ahab’s meticulous chart‑work and cetological ambition, creating a world where irrational belief and empirical planning intertwine.
- The sea is a “terra incognita” of sublime beauty and existential threat: the whale‑line is a “halter round all men’s necks,” the lee shore represents spiritual peril, and “landlessness alone resides highest truth.”
- Embedded tales and sworn testimonies (the *Town‑Ho* story, historical ship‑sinkings) are marshalled to authenticate the narrative’s plausibility while simultaneously admitting their own implausibility and selective secrecy.
